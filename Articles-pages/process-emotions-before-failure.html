Process Your Own Emotions First, Before You Can Help Your Child Navigate Failure

Monday June 8, 2026

Translated excerpt from the original by Johnny C.

This story is about myself. When I first opened the board game club, I made the exact same mistake.

Once, a young player I knew very well lost a game. I had watched him grow up; he was usually smart and sharp, but in that particular game, he completely lost his touch and suffered a pretty bad defeat.

I walked over, patted him on the shoulder, and said:

“It's okay. It's just a game, losing is part of it.”

But as I said it, I actually felt a knot of frustration in my chest—I felt he could have won. He clearly had the skills.

Later, one of the teachers at the club told me: “Johnny, when you said 'it's okay,' your chin was jutted forward, and your voice was half a pitch higher than usual. As the kid was leaving, he whispered to his mom, 'Johnny seems upset.'”

Hearing that, my heart sank.

It turned out that I, too, was saying one thing while my body expressed another.

Since then, I started practicing one thing: managing my own emotions before the child loses.

Take a deep breath, tuck my chin back, and let my voice drop. Then ask myself: What do I actually care about right now? The child's win or loss, or my own ego?

I am still practicing this today.

It's hard. But it's worth it.

Because only when I genuinely believe “it's okay” inside, will the “it's okay” that I speak actually reach the child's heart.

🍊 A Final Note

Over the past four years, I've seen far too many parents whose mouths say “it's okay,” while their faces scream “it matters a lot.”

It's not that they don't love their children; they are just terrified of seeing them face setbacks.

But what they don't realize is that a child's greatest setback isn't losing a board game. It's discovering that the person they trust the most doesn't trust them to handle a loss.

So, the next time your child loses, please—

Don't open your mouth right away.

Take a deep breath, relax your shoulders, smooth out your furrowed brow, and let your face be the first to say, “It's okay.”

Then, speak.

You will find that your voice naturally becomes gentle.

Because children don't just listen to what you say; they watch how you say it.
