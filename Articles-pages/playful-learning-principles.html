How to Turn Play into Learning: 5 Principles of Playful Learning

Monday June 8, 2026

Have you ever noticed how children completely transform when they are in the driver's seat of their own game? It turns out, there is profound academic science behind this everyday observation.

Researchers at the Harvard Graduate School of Education's Project Zero have spent years studying the Pedagogy of Play (PoP). Their goal is simple but ambitious: to bridge the gap between free play and formal schooling. Interestingly, the practical lessons we observe in everyday settings—like a local children's board game club—often echo these research findings.

Whether you are a parent, a teacher, or someone who wants to help kids thrive, here is how five practical, everyday observations map onto Project Zero's playful learning framework.

Principle 1: Give the Initiative Back to Children

Core Practice: Empower learners to lead their own learning.

In traditional schooling, adults almost always set the agenda. We decide what is learned, when it is learned, and how it is learned. However, the Pedagogy of Play emphasizes that during playful learning, children must be in charge.

To foster genuine engagement, educators and parents must tip the balance of responsibility toward the learners. Giving kids the initiative does not mean leaving them unsupervised; it means giving them the autonomy to make choices, experience the natural consequences of those choices, and own their learning process.

Principle 2: Protect Curiosity, Don't Rush to Give Answers

Core Practice: Encourage imaginative thinking and promote experimentation.

When a child asks a question or gets stuck, our adult instinct is often to swoop in and provide the correct answer. Project Zero researchers suggest a different approach: design open-ended investigations.

Curiosity is fragile. Instead of providing immediate solutions, we should embrace risk-taking and prompt children with questions like, "What do you think happened?" or "What if we tried it this way?" Curiosity is not built by memorizing answers; it thrives on the freedom to experiment.

Principle 3: Make Learning Experiential and Expressible

Core Practice: Build a culture of collaborative learning.

Learning should not just happen quietly in a child's head—it needs to be visible. The Project Zero framework focuses on making learning meaningful through tangible, hands-on experiences.

Furthermore, learning is solidified through purposeful conversation. When children are encouraged to articulate the rules of a game or reflect out loud on what they just experienced, they transition from merely absorbing information to truly understanding it. If they can express it, they own it.

Principle 4: Adults Change from "Lecturers" to "Supporters"

Core Practice: Navigate the paradoxes of play.

Researchers note that there is an inherent tension—a paradox—between the wild, free nature of play and the structured environment of traditional schooling.

To resolve this, adults must fundamentally shift their roles. Instead of acting as "lecturers" who deliver facts, we must act as a scaffold. This means standing close enough to provide support when a child is genuinely stuck and frustrated, but having the restraint to step back and let the child navigate the moment they find their footing.

Principle 5: Give Learning Through Play Enough Time and Trust

Core Practice: Foster trust and welcome negotiation.

The Pedagogy of Play framework highlights a challenging reality: "Play is timeless, but schools are timetabled." Deep, meaningful play requires a narrative arc. It needs setup, negotiation, peaks, and resolutions.

Building a playful culture requires adults to trust this messy process. We have to resist the urge to rush children to the finish line, giving learners the unhurried time they need to deeply engage. Sometimes, the slowest path is actually the fastest way to learn.

A Final Note for Adults

Translating academic theory into everyday practice does not require a degree in education. It simply requires a shift in perspective. By stepping back, trusting the process, and letting children take the lead, we can turn any everyday game into a masterclass in learning.
